## 3. How it learns

*Sources:* ../runs/baseline.log; ../runs/baseline/last.pt (logits, loss slice); ../research-log.md

**learn-1** — Those ten scores are logits. Any size, positive or negative. Not probabilities yet.

> Visual: Real logits for the frog as signed bars.

**learn-2** — Softmax turns them into probabilities. Exponentiate each one, then divide by the total. Now they're positive, and they sum to one.

> Visual: Softmax equation; bars morph into real probabilities.

**learn-3** — For this frog, the baseline bets mostly on frog, but not all in.

> Visual: Frog probability highlighted.

**learn-4** — Cross-entropy is the negative log of the probability on the right answer. Confident and right costs almost nothing. Confident and wrong costs a lot.

> Visual: L = −log p_y curve with the real frog point.

**learn-5** — One detail. The network itself only outputs logits. PyTorch's cross-entropy applies log-softmax internally. Softmax here is for reading outputs, not an extra layer.

> Visual: nn.CrossEntropyLoss(z, y) = −log softmax(z)_y; no softmax layer in build_model.

**learn-6** — Backpropagation uses the chain rule to send that loss backwards, telling every parameter which way would lower it.

> Visual: Forward particles, then gradient pulses flow backward; chain-rule equation.

**learn-7** — This is a real slice of the loss around our trained baseline, along two random directions in weight space. Gradient descent steps downhill. The path drawn here is illustrative, not recorded steps.

> Visual: Measured 25x25 loss slice (tools/extract_static.py); path labelled illustrative; update rule w ← w − η∇L.

**learn-8** — Each step uses a batch of 64 images. One pass over the training set, an epoch, is 782 steps.

> Visual: 782 batch tiles fill up one epoch.

**learn-9** — Ten epochs at learning rate 0.01 gave our first baseline. 63.68 percent on test. Wrong more than a third of the time.

> Visual: Real per-epoch test curve from ../runs/baseline.log.

## 4. Faster isn't better

*Sources:* ../BENCHMARKS.md; ../runs/tuned-b512.log; ../runs/step-matched-b512.log

**batch-1** — First, we tried going faster. At batch 512, ten epochs took 5 seconds instead of 22.

> Visual: Two lanes: batch 64 vs batch 512; timers 22.39 s vs 5.21 s.

**batch-2** — But accuracy fell to 48.58 percent. Turns out bigger batches mean fewer steps. 980 updates instead of 7,820.

> Visual: Step dots: 7,820 vs 980; illustrative step paths (labelled) on the measured loss slice.

**batch-3** — With 80 epochs, 7,840 updates, almost exactly matched, it reached 67.67 percent.

> Visual: Real test curves plotted against optimizer updates.

**batch-4** — But each step used eight times as many images. Bigger batches aren't magic. They trade more work per step for fewer steps.

> Visual: Image-pass bars: 0.5M vs 4M.

## 5. Augmentation, round one

*Sources:* ../runs/augmentation/comparison.json + comparison.md; ../augmentations.py

**aug1-1** — Next, augmentation. Show a variation of each photo instead of the exact same one. Flip it, or pad the border and crop a shifted window.

> Visual: Real horse: flip, pad with 4 black pixels, crop windows.

**aug1-2** — A horse facing left is still a horse. This teaches invariance, and makes memorizing single images harder, a form of regularization.

> Visual: All views map to one label.

**aug1-3** — We tested 15 recipes, three seeds each, on the small network. No augmentation, 67.96 percent. Flip helped a little, 68.77. Crop plus flip hurt, 66.38.

> Visual: Real bar chart of all 15 recipes with seed error bars.

**aug1-4** — The subtle part. Crop plus flip shrank the train-test gap from about 7 points to under 1. But a smaller gap can mean useful regularization, or a model that just learned less. Test accuracy fell, so the gap alone isn't evidence of improvement.

> Visual: Real train/test curves; gap 7.33 vs 0.74 points.

**aug1-5** — This suite also checked the test set repeatedly, so treat it as exploratory.

## 8. Residual connections

*Sources:* ../depth_cnn.py (Block.shortcut zero-pad); ../runs/depth/comparison.json; https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385

**residual-1** — A residual block keeps the same two convolutions, but adds a shortcut that skips around them and gets added back at the end.

> Visual: Plain block and residual block side by side; shortcut draws.

**residual-2** — So the block computes F of x. Conv, batch norm, ReLU, conv, batch norm. And the output is ReLU of F of x, plus the shortcut.

> Visual: y = ReLU(F(x) + shortcut(x)).

**residual-3** — When channels grow, our shortcut is padded with zeros. No learned weights, so each residual network matches its plain twin's parameters exactly.

> Visual: 32 channels + 32 zero channels → 64.

**residual-4** — Why might that help? A block that should do nearly nothing just pushes F toward zero, instead of learning to copy its input. And in backprop, the shortcuts give gradients a direct route. Schematic, not measured.

> Visual: Vector diagram x + F(x) = y, then gradient pulses on both paths (schematic).

**residual-5** — Matched seeds, identical starting weights. Residual 4, 83.77. Eight, 86.67. Sixteen, 86.85. Thirty-two, 87.20.

> Visual: Real residual line joins the plain line.

**residual-6** — At 32 layers, the shortcut matters, 83.08 up to 87.20. But it didn't beat plain at 4, 8, or 16, and it's only 0.03 points above plain 16, inside the seed spread. On test, it scored 86.43 percent, plus or minus 0.49.

> Visual: Gap at 32 highlighted; spread band; test card.

## 10. Building ResNet-18

*Sources:* ../followup_cnn.py (build_model); torchvision 0.22.1 resnet18; ../runs/followup/resnet18-crop-flip/seed-*/metrics.jsonl

**resnet-1** — Finally, ResNet-18, adapted for CIFAR and trained from random weights. No ImageNet pre-training.

> Visual: Empty pipeline; 'weights=None'.

**resnet-2** — The stem is one 3 by 3 convolution, 3 to 64 channels, stride 1. ImageNet's 7 by 7 stride 2 stem and max pool would crush a 32 by 32 image to 8 by 8, so we swapped one and dropped the other.

> Visual: Stem slab; ghosted ImageNet stem crossed out.

**resnet-3** — Then eight basic residual blocks, two per stage, at 64, 128, 256, and 512 channels. Each stage halves resolution. 32, 16, 8, 4. Where shapes change, the shortcut is a learned 1 by 1 convolution with batch norm.

> Visual: Blocks appear stage by stage; amber projection shortcuts.

**resnet-4** — Global average pooling turns each 4 by 4 map into one number, and one linear layer maps 512 features to ten classes. No hidden 128 layer.

> Visual: 512x4x4 → 512 → 10.

**resnet-5** — Why 18? The stem plus 16 block convolutions is 17, and the classifier makes 18. By convention, shortcut projections don't count.

> Visual: Counter 1 + 16 + 1 = 18.

**resnet-6** — So it's not just layer count. It keeps resolution longer, has far more channels, about 11.2 million parameters, nine times residual 32, and pools globally. And it trained 200 epochs, not 160. A recipe comparison, not a clean architecture test.

> Visual: Side-by-side comparison card.

**resnet-7** — Across three seeds, it averaged 95.77 percent validation accuracy over the last ten epochs, plus or minus 0.11. We score the final ten epochs, never the single best one.

> Visual: Real ResNet-18 validation curve vs residual-32 crop+flip; final-10 window.
